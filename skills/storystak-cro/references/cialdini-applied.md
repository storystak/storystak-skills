# Cialdini's Principles, Applied to Landing Pages

> **Purpose:** Robert Cialdini's six (now seven) principles of persuasion are foundational to CRO. But the *application* of each principle differs wildly by industry. "Authority" on a B2B SaaS page looks completely different from "Authority" on a DTC supplement page. This reference shows how to deploy each principle with industry-specific examples.

---

## The Seven Principles

1. **Reciprocity** — People feel obligated to return favors
2. **Commitment & Consistency** — People want to act consistently with their prior commitments
3. **Social Proof** — People look to others' actions to decide their own
4. **Authority** — People defer to credible experts
5. **Liking** — People are persuaded by people they like, trust, and identify with
6. **Scarcity** — People value what's limited or hard to get
7. **Unity** — People are persuaded by shared identity (added in *Pre-Suasion*, 2016)

Used ethically — not as manipulation, but as ways to communicate genuine value, real proof, and authentic differentiation — these principles help honest offers convert at higher rates.

---

## Principle 1: Reciprocity

### The Core Idea
When someone gives you something of value, you feel obligated to give back. On landing pages, this manifests as offering free value upfront before asking for anything in return.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- Free trial with no credit card → company "gives" trial access; visitor "gives" eventual conversion
- Free templates / spreadsheets / calculators → builds trust before sales conversation
- Free audit / assessment / report → high-value deliverable in exchange for contact info

**DTC E-commerce**
- Free shipping over $X → triggers reciprocity at checkout
- Free sample with first order → especially effective for trial-able categories
- Free guide / quiz with personalized recommendations → educational value before product pitch

**High-Ticket Services / Coaching**
- Free strategy call / discovery call → consultant gives time and expertise upfront
- Free framework / playbook / case study → proves value before asking for paid engagement
- Free webinar / workshop → 60–90 minutes of value before any pitch

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Free physical mailed kit → tangible value, especially powerful for older audiences
- Free assessment / qualification quiz → builds reciprocity through personalization
- Free guide / report → educational value before sales conversation

**Info Products**
- Free training video / webinar → reciprocity engine for entire info-product industry
- Free PDF / cheat sheet → low-cost reciprocity trigger
- Free trial of paid community → "join free for 7 days"

### Watch-Outs
- The "free" offer must have actual value — fake or low-quality lead magnets damage reciprocity
- Don't pitch immediately after delivering — let the value land
- Compliance: in some industries, "free" claims are regulated (FTC guidelines on what qualifies as truly free)

## Principle 2: Commitment & Consistency

### The Core Idea
Once people make a small commitment, they want to act consistently with it. On landing pages, this manifests as multi-step funnels, micro-yes ladders, and progressive disclosure.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- Multi-step demo request: "What's your role?" → "Company size?" → "What problem are you solving?" → contact info
- Free trial signup that requires light initial commitment (email + password) before deeper onboarding
- ROI calculator that asks the visitor to input their data → they're now invested

**DTC E-commerce**
- "Find your shade / size / fit" quiz → personalized recommendations after 5–7 commitments
- Subscribe-and-save with cancel-anytime → small commitment to recurring purchase
- Wishlist / save-for-later → builds attachment before purchase

**High-Ticket Services**
- Application-based intake → "see if you qualify" requires commitment to answer questions
- Discovery call booking → calendar selection is a commitment to show up
- Diagnostic assessment → committing to assessment commits to discussing results

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Quiz funnel: "Do you currently own a [thing]?" → "How long?" → "What's your biggest frustration?" → contact info
- Each question is an easy "yes" — by the time the contact-info ask comes, they're invested

**Info Products**
- Webinar registration → showing up is a commitment → buying the offer at the end is consistent with prior commitment
- "Choose your path" intake → commits the visitor to a track before the offer is presented

### Watch-Outs
- Don't manufacture commitments that aren't real (fake quizzes that route everyone to the same offer)
- Each step should feel valuable to the visitor, not just like a hoop to jump through
- Mobile: each step must be effortless (button taps, not typing) or commitment ladder breaks

## Principle 3: Social Proof

### The Core Idea
People look at what others are doing to decide what they should do. Social proof is the most-used and most-tested CRO principle for a reason.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- Customer logo bar (recognized brands)
- "Used by [N] teams"
- G2 / Capterra ratings + badges
- Named case studies with metrics
- "Top of [category]" awards

**DTC E-commerce**
- Star ratings + total review count (most-used CRO element)
- UGC (user-generated content) — real customer photos
- "X people viewing this right now" (real-time social proof)
- "Y purchased today" (volume signal)
- Influencer endorsements (with FTC disclosures)

**High-Ticket Services**
- Named client testimonials with specific outcomes
- Video testimonials from named clients
- Logos of past clients (if approved)
- Case study count or revenue impact ("$X in client outcomes")

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Aggregate trust numbers ("58,583+ families helped")
- Real reviews from real people (with strict compliance redaction)
- Volume-based proof ("8,000+ reviews")
- Industry badges (BBB, certifications)

**Info Products**
- Student / community size ("Join 5,000+ students")
- Testimonials from successful students
- "Most popular course in [category]" (if true)
- Earnings disclaimers + success stories (compliance-permitting)

### Watch-Outs
- Social proof must be real — fake reviews and inflated numbers are detectable and damaging
- Match the social proof type to audience trust expectations (B2B wants logos; consumer wants UGC)
- Compliance: testimonials often require disclaimers ("results not typical") in regulated categories

## Principle 4: Authority

### The Core Idea
People defer to credible experts. The challenge is that "authority" looks completely different by industry — what signals expertise to a CFO doesn't signal expertise to a 70-year-old timeshare owner.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- SOC 2 Type II certification, ISO certifications
- Engineering team credentials (ex-Google, ex-Stripe, etc.)
- Detailed technical documentation
- Open-source contributions, GitHub presence
- Speaking at industry conferences (logos: SaaStr, Dreamforce, etc.)
- Customer logos that signal "we're trusted by serious teams"

**DTC E-commerce**
- Founder credentials (former exec at recognizable brand, MD, PhD, etc.)
- "Featured in [press]" badge bar
- Manufacturing transparency (lab tested, third-party verified, made in USA)
- Industry certifications (USDA Organic, Leaping Bunny, B Corp)
- Founder appears in product photos / videos

**High-Ticket Services / Coaching**
- Specific credentials (years in industry, named past clients, revenue scaled)
- Books published, podcast appearances, conference talks
- Founder's specific track record ("Sold three companies before this one")
- Methodology origin story ("Developed at [recognizable institution]")

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Founder origin story ("Industry insider turned advocate")
- Years in business + specific client volume
- Specific certifications (Inc. 500, BBB, industry-specific)
- Press mentions (60 Minutes, NYT, etc.)
- Specific founder credentials matched to the problem

**Info Products / Coaching**
- Outcome-based authority ("Built and sold $X company")
- Specific track record with named outcomes
- Industry recognition / awards
- Endorsements from recognized peers

### Watch-Outs
- Authority signals must be specific — "industry expert" means nothing; "20 years building accounts payable systems at Oracle and Stripe" is specific
- Match authority signal to audience trust expectations
- Compliance: avoid implying credentials you don't have (esp. medical, legal, financial — these have professional licensing rules)

## Principle 5: Liking

### The Core Idea
People are persuaded by people they like, trust, and identify with. On landing pages, this manifests as relatable founder voices, customer-mirror testimonials, and brand voice that matches the audience.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- Founder-led video on homepage (humanizes the brand)
- "From the team" voice (not corporate "we are pleased to announce")
- Customer testimonials from people who look/sound like the buyer
- Conversational support content, blog posts, documentation

**DTC E-commerce**
- Founder photos and personal stories
- UGC that shows real customers (not models)
- Brand voice that matches audience (irreverent for younger; warm for older)
- Behind-the-scenes content, "meet the team," manufacturing tours

**High-Ticket Services**
- Founder-led: founder photos, voice, video on every key page
- Personal stories that connect founder's experience to client's experience
- Conversational tone, not corporate
- Real names, real photos for the team page

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Founder origin story is often THE highest-leverage liking element
- Real customer photos and full names in testimonials (with permission)
- Warm, approachable brand voice — closer to "friend warning you" than "company selling you"

**Info Products / Coaching**
- Heavy founder presence on every page
- Personal vulnerability — founder shares their own struggle and journey
- Audience-mirror language — speaks to the audience as a peer who's been there

### Watch-Outs
- Brand voice must match audience expectations — too formal with consumer audiences; too casual with enterprise audiences
- Authentic vulnerability beats manufactured "founder story" framing
- Real photos of real people (founder, team, customers) outperform stock photography in nearly every test

## Principle 6: Scarcity

### The Core Idea
People value what's limited or hard to get. On landing pages, this manifests as cohort limits, cart-open windows, inventory scarcity, and selectivity framing.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- Beta access ("Join the waitlist — 200 spots in this cohort")
- Pricing increase warnings ("Lock in pricing before [date]")
- Limited-time bonuses ("Sign up by Friday for [bonus]")

**DTC E-commerce**
- Real inventory scarcity ("Only 47 left")
- Drop windows ("This collection drops Friday")
- Limited-edition variants
- "Selling out" badges on product cards

**High-Ticket Services**
- Cohort limits ("12 clients per quarter — currently 3 spots open")
- Application windows ("Applications open until [date]")
- Geographic scarcity ("Only available in [city]")
- Capacity limits ("We can only onboard [N] new clients per month")

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- Selectivity bar ("We accept 1 in 4 applicants" — see Principle 6 below for selectivity-as-Cialdini)
- Time-bound free offers ("Free [thing] this week only")

**Info Products**
- Cart-open windows (hard deadline) — entire info-product industry runs on this
- Bonuses that expire ("Sign up by Friday for $X in bonuses")
- Cohort starts ("Next cohort begins [date]")

### Watch-Outs
- **NEVER fake scarcity.** Real scarcity (inventory, cohort, timeline) works; fake scarcity (countdown timers that reset, "only 3 left" when you have 10,000) is detectable and destroys trust
- Compliance: some scarcity claims are regulated (FTC on inventory claims, especially "going fast" / "limited stock")
- The scarcity should match the offer logic — a SaaS with infinite inventory shouldn't claim "only 3 spots"

### Selectivity as Scarcity
A particularly effective form of scarcity: "We accept 1 in 4 applicants." This combines scarcity (limited acceptance) with authority (we have standards) and reciprocity (you're being given the privilege of being considered). Works especially well for high-ticket services and lead-gen for regulated services.

## Principle 7: Unity

### The Core Idea
People are persuaded by shared identity. On landing pages, this manifests as in-group framing, audience-specific language, and "built for [you specifically]" positioning.

### How It Plays by Industry

**B2B SaaS**
- "Built for [specific role/industry]" — "For finance teams at companies with 100–1,000 employees"
- Industry-specific case studies and language
- Persona-specific landing pages
- Industry-specific community ("Join the [vertical] CFO Slack")

**DTC E-commerce**
- Identity-based positioning — "For runners who've never owned a smartwatch they actually like"
- Community framing — "Join 50,000+ [identity] who…"
- Brand identity that signals tribe membership (specific aesthetic, specific values, specific in-group language)

**High-Ticket Services**
- "I work with founders who…" / "We help [specific role] who [specific situation]"
- Audience-mirror language: same vocabulary, same references, same pain points
- Founder is a member of the audience tribe (founder-led brands often beat corporate brands here)

**Lead-Gen for Regulated Services**
- "Built for [audience]" framing — "Help for timeshare owners who've tried everything else"
- Founder origin story creates unity (founder was in audience's shoes)

**Info Products**
- Tribe identity — "For [specific archetype] who want to [specific outcome]"
- In-group language and references
- Community/cohort framing

### Watch-Outs
- Unity-based positioning means picking a side — and that means alienating the other side. That's the trade-off, and it's usually correct (a page that's "for everyone" converts no one)
- Don't force unity that isn't real — fake "we get you" framing is detectable
- Match unity signals to actual audience identity (research the audience first, then position to it)

## Combining Principles

The strongest landing pages stack multiple principles in a single section. Examples:

### "We accept 1 in 4 applicants" stacks:
- **Scarcity** (limited acceptance)
- **Authority** (we have standards)
- **Reciprocity** (privilege of being considered)

### "Founded by a former debt collector who switched sides" stacks:
- **Authority** (insider credibility)
- **Liking** (personal story, vulnerability)
- **Unity** (was in the audience's shoes)

### "Used by Stripe, Notion, and 500+ finance teams. SOC 2 Type II certified." stacks:
- **Social Proof** (named customers, volume)
- **Authority** (certification, recognized brands)

### "Try free for 30 days. No credit card. Cancel anytime." stacks:
- **Reciprocity** (free value upfront)
- **Effort & Sacrifice reduction** (per Hormozi's Value Equation)
- **Risk reversal** (cancel anytime)

## Anti-Patterns (Avoid These)

- ❌ **Fake scarcity** — fake countdown timers, inflated "only X left" claims
- ❌ **Fake testimonials** — placeholder reviews, AI-generated reviews, paid reviews without disclosure
- ❌ **Manufactured authority** — claiming credentials you don't have
- ❌ **Manipulative reciprocity** — "free" lead magnets that are clearly low-effort PDFs designed to harvest emails
- ❌ **Forced unity** — "we get you" framing without actually understanding the audience
- ❌ **Single-principle reliance** — pages that lean on only one principle (only social proof, only authority) underperform pages that stack multiple
- ❌ **Stacking too many principles in one element** — "Limited spots! Trusted by 500+! Founded by ex-Google! Free trial!" reads as desperate; spread principles across sections
